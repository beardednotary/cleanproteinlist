Consumer Reports' Protein Lead Report, One Year Later: What Actually Changed

Published October 14, 2026 | Original Research & Analysis

Direct Answer: What changed in the year since Consumer Reports' protein lead report?

The courts moved and the legislature didn't. Since Consumer Reports published its lead testing on October 14, 2025, at least seven proposed class actions have been filed against protein brands and retailers. California's SB 1033 testing-and-disclosure bill stalled in committee in May 2026 and did not become law. None of the products named in the suits has been recalled. A year ago today, Consumer Reports told protein buyers that most of the powders and shakes it tested carried more lead per serving than it considers safe to consume daily. This site was built around that report. One year on, here's what actually happened, sticking to what we can document.

What Consumer Reports Found in October 2025

Consumer Reports tested 23 protein powders and ready-to-drink shakes for lead and other heavy metals. Its benchmark is a daily level of concern of 0.5 µg of lead, the same figure California uses for Prop 65 warnings. The headline results:

  • More than two-thirds of the products exceeded that level in a single serving.
  • Plant-based proteins averaged about nine times more lead than dairy (whey) proteins.
  • The worst result, Naked Nutrition's Vegan Mass Gainer, measured 7.7 µg per serving, roughly 1,570% of the level of concern.
  • A handful of products, mostly whey, cleared it comfortably. MuscleTech's mass gainer had no detectable lead.

Consumer Reports added five more products in a January 2026 follow-up round. Every brand and result we track is in our full ranked list.

The Courts: Seven Class Actions in Twelve Months

The clearest consequence of the report has been litigation. Several complaints cite Consumer Reports' findings directly, and the first was filed within weeks of publication.

Three patterns stand out:

  • The target shifted from brands to retailers. The two most recent suits name Costco and Amazon rather than Orgain itself, on the theory that the seller had a duty to disclose what testing could detect.
  • Lead isn't the only metal. One of the two Jocko suits is about cadmium alone, and both Orgain suits allege lead, cadmium and arsenic.
  • Nothing has been decided. As of today we found no ruling on the merits, settlement or certified class in any of these cases. Costco has moved to dismiss, arguing that "clean" marketing is puffery rather than a promise of no heavy metals. The judge hasn't ruled.
Editorial note: These are allegations in pending lawsuits. They are unproven, and none of the plaintiffs claims a physical injury; the cases are about labeling and disclosure. The companies named dispute the claims or have not responded publicly. Nothing here is legal advice.

The Legislature: SB 1033 Stalled

California came closest to turning the report into law. SB 1033, introduced by Sen. Steve Padilla on February 11, 2026 and co-sponsored by Consumer Reports and the Environmental Working Group, would have required protein makers to test for heavy metals, publish the results, and stop selling noncompliant products in California from January 1, 2028.

  • March 25, 2026: passed the Senate Health Committee 10–0.
  • April 22, 2026: passed the Senate Environmental Quality Committee.
  • May 4, 2026: placed on the Senate Appropriations suspense file.
  • May 14, 2026: held in committee and under submission.

"Held under submission" on the suspense file is how California bills typically stop moving without a recorded vote against them. 2026 was the second year of the 2025–26 session, so SB 1033 did not advance this session. It would need to be reintroduced in 2027. There is still no federal or state law requiring heavy-metal testing or disclosure for protein powder.

2. From "is mine safe?" to "what's cleanest?"

Behavior shifted along with awareness:

  • The share whose main concern was "Show me the absolute cleanest proteins" rose from 11% to 20%. "Is my current brand safe?" is still the most common answer, but it fell from 78% to 68%.
  • The share who said "I'm just researching brands", rather than reporting a current routine, rose from 8% to 21%.

Taken together: fewer readers are checking a brand they already use, and more are comparing options before they buy. These subgroups are smaller (Jan–Apr n=207, May–Oct n=153), so treat them as direction rather than precise figures.

Compare Jocko Fuel. Two lawsuits were filed against it in January and February 2026, yet Jocko never reached even 1.5% of respondents in any month through September. That's worth remembering when brands argue litigation alone is driving consumer concern.

What Hasn't Changed

  • Protein powder is still a dietary supplement. The FDA doesn't review or routinely test it before it's sold, and manufacturers aren't required to publish heavy-metal results.
  • "Organic" still says nothing about metals. USDA Organic covers farming practices. Plant proteins draw lead and cadmium from soil whether or not they're organic.
  • Third-party certification is still the best signal on the label. NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport and Clean Label Project certification all include heavy-metal testing. That was the practical advice a year ago, and with no testing law in place, it still is.

Methodology & Limits

  • Who answered: visitors to CleanProteinList.com, who mostly arrive from searches about lead and brand safety. They are far more safety-minded than protein buyers overall. Read the figures as trends within that group, not national estimates.
  • No pre-report baseline: the quiz began January 23, 2026 and the brand poll question was added January 21, 2026, both three months after the report. We can show change over 2026, not a before-and-after comparison around October 2025.
  • Deduplication: repeat submissions of the same answer within six minutes are counted once (quiz: 380 raw, 360 deduplicated). Poll brand names are normalized for spelling and product-line variants.
  • Sample sizes: quiz awareness, n=188 (Jan–Apr) and n=136 (May–Oct 12); poll monthly n from 155 to 553. Site traffic fell in late summer, so recent months carry smaller samples. No data was collected September 14–16, when the site was briefly unreachable.
  • No incentives were offered, and no brand has paid to appear in or influence any of this data.

FAQ

Q: Did California pass the protein testing law?

A: No. SB 1033 passed two Senate committees but was held in the Appropriations Committee on May 14, 2026 and did not advance this session. It would have taken effect January 1, 2028.

Q: How many lawsuits followed the Consumer Reports report?

A: We count at least seven proposed class actions between October 2025 and July 2026, against Naked Nutrition, Garden of Life, Jocko Fuel (twice), Quest Nutrition, Costco and Amazon. None had been decided or settled as of October 14, 2026.

Q: Has any of these protein powders been recalled?

A: No. The lawsuits are civil claims about labeling and disclosure, not FDA recalls, and the allegations are unproven.

Q: Is whey still safer than plant protein for lead?

A: On Consumer Reports' data, yes: plant proteins averaged about nine times more lead than whey. Certified plant options exist, but there are fewer of them. See our lead-free rankings.

The Bottom Line

A year after Consumer Reports' report, accountability for lead in protein powder is coming through the courts, not the legislature. Seven class actions are pending, California's testing bill stalled, and nothing requires brands to test or disclose. Readers aren't waiting: among people researching this topic, awareness of the testing nearly doubled, and more of them are comparing options before they buy. Until disclosure is required, third-party certification remains the most useful thing to look for on the label.
